# Key Considerations for the §9.60 AOT Hearing

## I. Statutory Framework & Burden of Proof

**Clear and convincing evidence standard** (Mental Hygiene Law §9.60(c)) — the highest civil standard. You must find:

1. **Serious and persistent mental illness** (SPMI) — Maya's nine-year schizophrenia history clearly satisfies this
2. **Likely to benefit from AOT** — requires individualized analysis, not assumption
3. **Unlikely to voluntarily comply** with treatment — critical disputed element
4. **Substantial probability** that without AOT she will suffer serious harm (§9.60(c)(iv)):
   - Serious bodily injury to self/others, OR
   - Serious and persistent inability to provide for food, clothing, shelter, or medical care

## II. Critical Evidentiary Gaps & Weaknesses in the Petition

**Dr. Patel's affidavit is stale:**
- Last examination: January 9, 2026 (3.5 months before hearing)
- Last LAI dose: same date (12 weeks before §9.41 episode)
- No direct observation of Maya's current mental state, functioning, or insight
- No assessment of her current willingness to engage in treatment
- Affidavits should be recent and based on current clinical contact

**The §9.41 episode alone may be insufficient:**
- CPEP attending determined she did NOT meet §9.39 criteria (imminent danger)
- Being outside without a coat in 38°F weather + talking to no one visible = concerning but not necessarily "serious harm" threshold
- No evidence of violence, self-harm, or inability to meet basic needs at that moment
- One episode after 12 weeks off medication ≠ automatic "serious harm" finding

**Missing current clinical information:**
- No assessment since January 9
- No recent medication compliance history
- No current functional status, housing stability, or support system assessment
- No evidence of what happened between Jan 9 and April 5 (3+ months)
- No recent psychiatric examination by the petitioner's clinician

## III. The "Unlikely to Voluntarily Comply" Element

This is likely **your most contested issue**:

**Petitioner's burden:**
- Must show Maya has a pattern of non-compliance or current refusal
- One missed appointment/clinic closure ≠ non-compliance
- Prior hospitalizations don't automatically prove future non-compliance
- Must distinguish between *inability* to access care vs. *unwillingness*

**Defense arguments (anticipate):**
- Clinic closure was structural barrier, not Maya's choice
- No evidence Maya refused treatment when offered
- No recent contact to assess current willingness
- Mother's petition may reflect caregiver burden, not clinical necessity
- Maya may be willing to engage if treatment is accessible

**Your inquiry should focus on:**
- What specific instances demonstrate non-compliance?
- Has Maya been offered treatment since January and refused?
- What is her current insight into her condition?
- Does she acknowledge need for treatment?
- What barriers exist to voluntary treatment?

## IV. The "Serious Harm" Threshold

**Statutory definition requires substantial probability of:**

- **Serious bodily injury** — no evidence of violence or self-harm attempts
- **Serious and persistent inability to provide for food, clothing, shelter, or medical care** — the April 5 episode suggests possible neglect, but:
  - She lives with her mother (housing stable)
  - No evidence of malnutrition, homelessness, or medical neglect
  - One incident of being outside without a coat ≠ "persistent inability"

**Critical distinction:** Relapse risk or symptom exacerbation ≠ "serious harm" under §9.60. The statute requires a higher threshold than mere clinical deterioration.

## V. Procedural & Constitutional Considerations

**Right to be present & confront evidence:**
- Is Maya present at the hearing?
- Can she testify about her current condition, willingness to engage, and circumstances?
- MHLS will likely cross-examine Dr. Patel on staleness of affidavit and lack of recent contact

**Least restrictive alternative:**
- Has voluntary treatment been attempted since clinic closure?
- Are there accessible outpatient options (community mental health center, telehealth)?
- Could intensive case management + voluntary medication work?
- AOT is coercive; you must find it necessary, not merely convenient

**Due process:**
- Ensure adequate notice and opportunity to be heard
- Ensure Maya understands the hearing and can participate meaningfully
- Consider whether she needs a competency evaluation if she cannot assist in her defense

## VI. Questions to Pose During the Hearing

**To Dr. Patel (or whoever testifies for petitioner):**
1. When was your last direct clinical contact with Maya? What was her mental status?
2. Have you assessed her since January 9? If not, why not?
3. What specific instances demonstrate she is "unlikely to voluntarily comply"?
4. Has she been offered treatment since January and refused?
5. What is her current insight into her illness?
6. The April 5 episode — what was her mental state? Was she a danger to herself or others?
7. What is the "serious harm" you anticipate? What is the timeline?
8. Have less restrictive alternatives been attempted?

**To Maya (if she testifies):**
1. Do you understand you have schizophrenia?
2. Do you believe you need psychiatric treatment?
3. Are you willing to take medication if it's available?
4. What happened to your outpatient care after January?
5. What were you doing on April 5? Why were you outside?
6. What would help you stay engaged in treatment?

## VII. Practical Considerations

**Timing & access:**
- The clinic closure is significant — it's a structural barrier, not Maya's fault
- If treatment is now accessible, voluntary engagement may be feasible
- AOT should not be a substitute for adequate community mental health infrastructure

**Family dynamics:**
- Mother's petition may reflect genuine concern OR caregiver burden/frustration
- Explore whether mother can support voluntary treatment
- Consider whether AOT might strain the therapeutic relationship

**Likelihood of compliance with AOT:**
- If Maya opposes AOT, forced compliance may be counterproductive
- Research shows AOT is most effective when combined with engagement and trust
- Consider whether coercion will help or harm long-term outcomes

## VIII. Your Decision Framework

**You should find AOT warranted only if you are convinced by clear and convincing evidence that:**

1. ✓ Maya has SPMI (schizophrenia) — **clearly met**
2. ? Maya is unlikely to voluntarily comply — **disputed; requires recent evidence**
3. ? Without AOT, substantial probability of serious harm — **questionable; one episode after 12 weeks off meds ≠ automatic serious harm**
4. ? AOT is likely to benefit her — **depends on her engagement and access to care**

**Red flags suggesting you should deny or defer:**
- Stale clinical information (3.5 months old)
- No recent assessment of current willingness to engage
- Structural barriers (clinic closure) rather than Maya's refusal
- Single episode after medication lapse, not pattern of non-compliance
- Accessible voluntary treatment not yet attempted

**Grounds for granting (if evidence supports):**
- Clear pattern of non-compliance despite access to care
- Recent clinical assessment showing current danger
- Specific, articulable serious harm (not speculative)
- AOT plan is realistic and accessible
- Less restrictive alternatives exhausted

---

## Bottom Line

This petition appears **premature and under-supported**. The stale affidavit, lack of recent clinical contact, and absence of evidence that Maya has *refused* treatment (vs. lost access to it) are significant weaknesses. The April 5 episode, while concerning, may not meet the "serious harm" threshold without more evidence of pattern or imminent danger.

**Consider whether the appropriate remedy is to deny the petition and order the petitioner to:**
- Reconnect Maya with accessible outpatient care
- Reassess in 30–60 days if she refuses voluntary treatment
- Refile with current clinical information if circumstances warrant

This protects Maya's liberty while preserving the option of AOT if voluntary treatment truly fails.
