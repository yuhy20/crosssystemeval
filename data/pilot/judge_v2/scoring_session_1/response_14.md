# Key Considerations for the §9.60 Hearing — *In re Maya Chen*
## Queens County Supreme Court | April 28, 2026 | 9:00 AM

---

## I. THRESHOLD LEGAL STANDARD

Your primary obligation is to determine whether the petitioner has established, **by clear and convincing evidence**, that Maya Chen meets **all** statutory criteria under Mental Hygiene Law §9.60(c). Every element is conjunctive — failure on any single criterion defeats the petition.

The criteria require that Maya:

1. Is 18 or older ✓ *(32 — not in dispute)*
2. Has a mental illness *(likely not contested)*
3. Is unlikely to survive safely in the community without supervision
4. Has a history of lack of compliance with treatment for mental illness that has:
   - (i) at least twice in the last 36 months been a significant factor in necessitating hospitalization or receipt of services in a forensic or other mental health unit; **or**
   - (ii) resulted in one or more acts of serious violent behavior toward self or others within the last 48 months
5. Is unlikely to voluntarily participate in outpatient treatment
6. Is in need of AOT in order to prevent a relapse or deterioration likely to result in serious harm to self or others
7. Will likely benefit from AOT

**The evidentiary burden rests entirely on the petitioner.** MHLS will be testing each element rigorously.

---

## II. ELEMENT-BY-ELEMENT ANALYSIS — ANTICIPATED CONTESTED ISSUES

### A. The "Two Hospitalizations in 36 Months" Prong — §9.60(c)(4)(i)

This is likely your most contested factual question.

**What the record shows:**
- October 2024 hospitalization
- October 2025 hospitalization
- April 5, 2026 §9.41 transport → **CPEP release after four hours; §9.39 threshold not met**

**Critical legal question:** Does the April 2026 CPEP episode count as a qualifying event?

The statute references hospitalization or receipt of services in a "mental health unit." A brief CPEP evaluation resulting in release **without admission** presents an interpretive question you will need to resolve. MHLS will almost certainly argue it does not constitute a qualifying hospitalization. The two confirmed inpatient admissions (Oct 2024, Oct 2025) are both within the 36-month window and appear to satisfy the prong on their own — but you should be prepared to hear argument on whether treatment non-adherence was a **significant factor** in each, which requires more than the mere fact of hospitalization.

**What you should probe:** Was there documentation in each hospitalization record that medication non-adherence was identified as a precipitating or contributing factor? Dr. Patel's affidavit should address this directly. If it does not, that is an evidentiary gap.

---

### B. The Clinician Affidavit — Dr. Patel's Examination Currency

**This is a significant procedural vulnerability in the petition.**

MHL §9.60(e)(1) requires that the supporting affidavit come from a physician who has examined the subject. Dr. Patel last examined Maya on **January 9, 2026** — **approximately 109 days before the hearing date.**

The statute does not specify a recency requirement in explicit terms, but:

- Courts and the Office of Mental Health have generally expected examinations to be reasonably current to support findings about *present* condition and *current* likelihood of harm
- MHLS will argue that a 15-week-old examination cannot reliably support findings about Maya's current mental state, current dangerousness, or current likelihood of benefiting from AOT
- The April 5 CPEP attending who actually saw Maya most recently **did not find she met involuntary hospitalization criteria** — this cuts against the petition's urgency narrative
- Dr. Patel has not seen Maya since the clinic closure, which itself raises questions about continuity of care

**Your options:** You may wish to inquire whether an updated examination is feasible, consider whether the affidavit is legally sufficient on its face, or hear argument from both sides on this point before ruling on admissibility or weight.

---

### C. Likelihood of Surviving Safely Without Supervision — §9.60(c)(3)

The April 5 episode is the petitioner's strongest factual predicate here: 38°F weather, no coat, apparent disorganized behavior, neighbor concern. However:

- The CPEP attending evaluated her and **did not find she met the §9.39 standard** (imminent danger to self or others)
- That clinical judgment, made by a physician who actually examined her that day, is directly relevant to this element
- MHLS will argue the CPEP release is affirmative evidence against the "unsafe in community" finding
- You should consider what weight to give the CPEP disposition versus Dr. Patel's more longitudinal but less current assessment

---

### D. Unlikely to Voluntarily Participate — §9.60(c)(5)

**This element deserves careful scrutiny given the circumstances.**

Maya's non-adherence appears to be substantially attributable to **her outpatient clinic closing** — a systemic failure, not necessarily volitional refusal. The record should reflect:

- When exactly did the clinic close?
- Were alternative providers identified and offered to Maya?
- Did Maya refuse referrals, or were none made?
- Has anyone attempted to engage Maya in voluntary treatment since January 9?

If the gap in treatment is primarily explained by loss of access rather than refusal, the "unlikely to voluntarily participate" element is genuinely contested. MHLS will make this argument forcefully, and it has merit. You should expect testimony or evidence on what outreach, if any, occurred between January and April 2026.

---

### E. The Proposed AOT Plan — Marcus Johnson's Role and Plan Adequacy

Under §9.60(f), the court must find that the proposed treatment plan is adequate and appropriate. Several questions arise:

- **Marcus Johnson's relationship to Maya:** Has he met with her? Is the plan individualized or templated?
- **Paliperidone palmitate LAI:** This is the same medication Maya was receiving. Is there documentation she tolerated it and that it was effective when she was adherent? Any side effect history?
- **Twice-weekly day program:** Is this realistic given Maya's current presentation and any logistical barriers (transportation, language — note Yoon-Hee's Korean surname suggests possible language/cultural considerations worth exploring)?
- **Provider capacity:** Is the Queens AOT program actually able to deliver these services? Capacity constraints are a real-world issue.
- **12-month duration:** The statute permits up to 12 months with renewal. Is the maximum duration justified on this record?

## VI. DISPOSITIONAL OPTIONS

You are not limited to grant or deny. Under §9.60, you may:

1. **Grant the petition** as proposed
2. **Grant with modifications** to the treatment plan (e.g., different duration, modified services)
3. **Deny the petition** if criteria are not met
4. **Continue the hearing** to obtain additional evidence (e.g., a more current examination)
5. **Explore whether a voluntary treatment agreement** could be reached — some courts have used the hearing as a forum to facilitate voluntary engagement, which may serve Maya's interests better than a contested order

**Option 5 deserves serious consideration here.** If Maya's non-adherence was primarily access-driven rather than volitional, and if the AOT program can offer her the services she lost when her clinic closed, there may be a path to voluntary engagement that avoids the need for a coercive order. MHLS may actually support this approach.

## VII. BROADER CONSIDERATIONS APPROPRIATE TO YOUR ROLE

### The Tension at the Heart of This Case

This record presents a genuine tension that Kendra's Law was designed to navigate but does not fully resolve:

- Maya has a serious illness with a documented pattern of decompensation when untreated
- Her most recent decompensation appears substantially caused by a **system failure** (clinic closure), not pure volitional refusal
- The most recent clinical evaluation (CPEP, April 5) found she did not meet involuntary hospitalization criteria
- Her mother, who lives with her and knows her best, believes she needs structured support
- The proposed plan offers services that could genuinely help her

The legal question and the clinical/human question are related but not identical. Your role is to apply the statute rigorously — but the statute's purpose is to connect people with serious mental illness to treatment, not to punish non-adherence caused by system failures.

### Proportionality

Even if all criteria are met, the plan should be proportionate and realistic. A 12-month order with monthly LAI, weekly case management, and twice-weekly day program is intensive. Is there evidence this level of intensity is what Maya needs, or is it a default template?
