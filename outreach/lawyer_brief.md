# What I'm asking you to help with

I'm a researcher working on how AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) handle legal questions when different professionals ask the same factual question. The hypothesis is that these tools sometimes apply *the wrong professional standard* — for example, giving someone a clinician's analysis when they asked a judge's question, or vice versa. I'd like an experienced lawyer's eyes on whether my measurement instrument actually catches that problem in the §9.60 / Kendra's Law context.

# What I'm not asking

I'm not asking for legal advice. I'm not asking you to opine on real cases. The fact pattern is fictional (a hypothetical petition for Maya Chen, a 32-year-old in Queens). I'm not asking you to be an expert witness or to do anything that would touch your real practice.

# What you'd actually do

I generated 6 responses from three different AI tools (Claude, ChatGPT, Llama) to the same hypothetical §9.60 hearing question. I wrote a 10-item scoring rubric for what a *good* response from a judge's standpoint would look like — items like "identifies §9.60(c) as the controlling test," "references the clear-and-convincing-evidence standard," "stays within the evidentiary record," etc.

I'd like you to:

1. **Read the rubric first.** If anchors are wrong or undergrad-level, tell me — that's the most valuable thing you can do, more than any score. I'd rather hear "this rubric is wrong about prong 4" than have you score against a flawed instrument.
2. **Score each of the 6 responses on each of the 10 items, 0–3.** Anchored descriptions for each score are in the rubric.
3. **Note any item where you disagreed with my anchor description** or where the response did something the rubric doesn't capture.
4. **Tell me at the end:** is this rubric a reasonable instrument for what it claims to measure?

A note on what you'll see: half the responses were generated from prompts that did *not* identify the user as a judge; they're the comparison point. Some of them read as petitioner's-counsel advice or generalist analysis rather than judge-perspective analysis. **Score them on the rubric anyway** — the rubric is supposed to ding them on impartiality / advocacy items, and that's exactly the data we need.

# What you'd produce

A Google Doc I'll share with you, containing the rubric and the 6 responses with score blanks. You fill in. We follow up with a 30-min call to walk through anything that surprised you.

# Time commitment

**60 minutes, hard cap.** Roughly 30 min reading the responses, 25 min scoring, 5 min on rubric-overall feedback. If at minute 20 you think the rubric is mis-conceived, stop scoring and just tell me — that's more valuable than finishing.
