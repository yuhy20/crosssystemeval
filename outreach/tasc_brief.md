# What I'm asking you to help with

I'm a researcher working on whether AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) shift their *professional standard* depending on who they think they're talking to. The concern is that the same factual question gets a clinician's analysis when a clinician asks, a lawyer's analysis when a lawyer asks, and a case-manager's analysis when a case-manager asks — but sometimes those answers cross wires, and you get e.g. clinical advice given to someone in a case-management role, or legal advice given to someone in a clinical role. The most common-sense intuition is "different professionals have different responsibilities, so AI should respect that." Whether AI tools actually *do* respect that is what I'm measuring.

# What I'm not asking

I'm not asking for advice about any real client. I'm not asking you to write or score a rubric — that's my job. I'm not asking you to certify anything for publication. The case in the materials (Maya Chen, fictional Queens resident, §9.60 / Kendra's Law) is made-up and is published in my public GitHub repo.

# What I'd like out of your hour

In the fact pattern, there's an LCSW named Marcus Johnson who works for the Queens AOT program. He drafted the proposed AOT plan that goes with the §9.60 petition. The role I want your expertise on is *his*: a clinical-liaison case-manager working with a court-mandated client.

Two things would be most useful:

1. **A critique of how the fact pattern describes his role.** I'll send a one-page excerpt. Tell me what's wrong, what's missing, what wouldn't happen this way in practice in NY. Even "this paragraph reads like someone who's never been in this job wrote it" is useful — that's the signal I need.
2. **Help seeding what a "good" answer from an AI tool would look like for someone in his role.** If a case-management-side LCSW asked an AI tool "what do I need to be careful about here?", what should that answer hit on, and what would a clinician's-standard or a lawyer's-standard answer look like by contrast (i.e., what would be the *wrong* answer)? I'm not asking you to write rubric items — I'm asking you to talk through what good and bad looks like from inside the role, and I'll do the writing afterward.

If we have time at the end, I'll show you one AI tool's actual response to a §9.60 question and ask what jumps out. That's a bonus, not the goal.

# What you do NOT need to do

- Read any legal text in advance. I'll bring the relevant statutory excerpt to the call.
- Score anything on a rubric. No 0-to-3 scales, no checklists.
- Comment on the lawyer-side rubric (a separate person is covering that).
- Speak to anything outside your direct experience. "I don't know, that's an AOT-program question, not a TASC question" is a complete and useful answer.

**60 minutes, hard cap.** Rough shape:

- 30 min — we talk through what good vs. bad case-management answers from an AI tool would look like
- 10 min — bonus: I show one actual AI response and you tell me what stands out

If at minute 20 something feels off — wrong framing, wrong person for the question, you're being asked to opine on something outside your role — say so and we stop. No hard feelings.
