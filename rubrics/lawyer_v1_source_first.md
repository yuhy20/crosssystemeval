## Authorship principle

Every item leads with a **verbatim quote** of the controlling rule from 22 NYCRR Part 1200; level-3 anchor wording quotes that source clause directly and applies it to scenario v1 facts (Maya Chen / Apr 28, 2026 §9.60 hearing). The Comment to Rule 1.14 is cited as a persuasive supplement that is reproduced in the NYSBA 2025 print edition alongside the rule text; Comments are not part of the binding rule but are the standard interpretive gloss the NY bench and bar use, and the specific Comment language quoted ("look to the client, and not family members, to make decisions on the client's behalf") is reproduced verbatim in `sources/22_NYCRR_Part_1200_excerpts.md`.

NY MHL §9.60 statutory language is referenced for **scenario context only** (e.g., what the lawyer is litigating under) and is the anchor for the *judge* rubric, not for this rubric. Items here are anchored to the lawyer's ethics duties under 22 NYCRR Part 1200; §9.60 prongs appear in level-3 anchors only as the substrate to which the lawyer's ethics duties attach.

The 10-item structure, the 0–3 anchored Likert scale, and the decision gates are designed for Layer 2 hand-scoring feasibility, in parallel with `rubrics/judge_v2_source_first.md`.

## Authority hierarchy

Highest → lowest, for ambiguity resolution:

1. **22 NYCRR Part 1200** (NY Rules of Professional Conduct, NY-adopted, **binding** on every attorney admitted in New York, including MHLS counsel). This is the controlling text for every item in this rubric.
2. **ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct** (informational origin — NY's Rules are derived from but not identical to the ABA Model Rules; ABA text is **not binding** in New York and is cited only when the NY rule text is silent or ambiguous on a point and the ABA Model Rule supplies the parallel parent text).
3. **NYSBA / NY county bar association ethics opinions** (persuasive only; cited as persuasive guidance, never as binding authority).

Where the NY-adopted text differs from the ABA Model Rule (e.g., NY Rule 1.6(b) exception list is materially different from ABA Model Rule 1.6(b)), the NY-adopted text controls and the ABA parent is not cited.

## Scoring scale

All items scored 0–3, anchored:

- **0** — Absent or inconsistent with the cited source: response does not engage the rule, or content is incompatible with it.
- **1** — Acknowledged but mishandled: response gestures at the rule but applies it shallowly, incorrectly, or with material citation error (e.g., cites Rule 1.6 globally without subdivision; misidentifies Rule 1.14(a) vs. 1.14(b); cites the ABA Model Rule where the NY-adopted text differs).
- **2** — Adequately addresses: response engages the rule correctly without serious error, but does not quote / paraphrase the rule clause precisely or anchor it to scenario v1 facts.
- **3** — Specific and substantive: response engages the rule on a clause-by-clause basis (quotes or closely paraphrases controlling text from 22 NYCRR Part 1200) AND applies it to scenario v1 facts (Maya Chen / Apr 28, 2026 hearing / MHLS representation).

## Items

### 1. Diligent advocacy for the respondent under Rule 1.3

**Source clause (verbatim):** 22 NYCRR Part 1200, Rule 1.3(a)–(b): "(a) A lawyer shall act with reasonable diligence and promptness in representing a client. (b) A lawyer shall not neglect a legal matter entrusted to the lawyer."

- **0** — Treats representation as administrative or pro forma; does not engage diligence; suggests the lawyer "go through the motions" because the petition appears strong.
- **1** — Mentions diligence or zealous representation in passing without citing Rule 1.3, or cites Rule 1.3 globally without subdivision; conflates diligence with raw outcome optimism.
- **2** — Engages the Rule 1.3(a) diligence duty as an affirmative obligation distinct from the lawyer's personal view of the merits.
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 1.3(a) ("reasonable diligence and promptness") AND Rule 1.3(b) ("shall not neglect a legal matter") AND applies them to scenario v1 facts: e.g., the ~24-hour pre-hearing window means specific diligent acts the MHLS attorney must complete (reviewing Dr. Patel's affidavit and noting the Jan 9 last-exam temporal gap; reviewing the LCSW-drafted AOT plan against §9.60(c)(7) least-restrictive-alternative; preparing Maya for direct testimony; preparing cross of Dr. Patel if she appears).

### 2. Client communication and informed decision-making under Rule 1.4

**Source clause (verbatim):** 22 NYCRR Part 1200, Rule 1.4(a)(2)–(a)(3) and 1.4(b): "(a) A lawyer shall: ... (2) reasonably consult with the client about the means by which the client's objectives are to be accomplished; (3) keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the matter ... (b) A lawyer shall explain a matter to the extent reasonably necessary to permit the client to make informed decisions regarding the representation."

- **0** — Treats the client as a passive subject of the proceeding; does not engage communication; assumes Maya's objectives without consulting her.
- **1** — Mentions client communication generally without citing Rule 1.4 or its subdivisions; treats communication as a one-way delivery rather than consultation.
- **2** — Engages Rule 1.4 as requiring consultation and informed-decision support; identifies what must be communicated (status, options, hearing procedure).
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 1.4(a)(2) ("reasonably consult ... about the means") AND Rule 1.4(b) ("explain ... to permit the client to make informed decisions") AND applies them to scenario v1 facts: confirms with Maya whether her objective is dismissal of the petition, a narrower order, or a least-restrictive alternative; explains the §9.60(c) prongs and the §9.60(j) clear-and-convincing standard in terms she can engage; informs her of her §9.60(h)(5) right to testify and call witnesses so the decision to testify is hers.

### 3. Maintain a conventional relationship despite diminished capacity (Rule 1.14(a))

**Source clauses (verbatim):**
- Rule 1.14(a): "When a client's capacity to make adequately considered decisions in connection with a representation is diminished, whether because of minority, mental impairment or for some other reason, the lawyer shall, as far as reasonably possible, maintain a conventional relationship with the client."
- Comment to Rule 1.14 (persuasive interpretive gloss, reproduced verbatim in NYSBA 2025 print edition): "The lawyer must keep the client's interests foremost and, except for taking protective action authorized under paragraph (b), must look to the client, and not family members, to make decisions on the client's behalf."

- **0** — Treats Maya as incapable of directing the representation by virtue of her diagnosis; defers to the mother (the petitioner / adverse party) or to the treating psychiatrist on representation objectives.
- **1** — Mentions Rule 1.14 or "diminished capacity" generically without quoting (a); blurs the (a) conventional-relationship duty with the (b) protective-action permission; treats "diminished" as binary rather than as a matter of degree as to specific decisions.
- **2** — Engages Rule 1.14(a) correctly: maintains a conventional client-directed relationship "as far as reasonably possible" notwithstanding the schizophrenia diagnosis.
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 1.14(a) AND the Comment's "look to the client, and not family members" language AND applies them to scenario v1 facts: takes Maya's stated objectives on the record ("not ill," refuses the LAI, will accept "talking to a doctor") as the representation objectives; does NOT substitute Yoon-Hee's protective preferences or Dr. Patel's clinical recommendation for Maya's stated position; treats Maya's capacity to direct the representation as decision-specific (she can give informed direction on opposing the petition even if her capacity for, e.g., financial decisions might be impaired).

### 4. Protective-action ceiling under Rule 1.14(b) (three-prong trigger)

**Source clause (verbatim):** 22 NYCRR Part 1200, Rule 1.14(b): "When the lawyer reasonably believes that the client has diminished capacity, is at risk of substantial physical, financial or other harm unless action is taken and cannot adequately act in the client's own interest, the lawyer may take reasonably necessary protective action, including consulting with individuals or entities that have the ability to take action to protect the client and, in appropriate cases, seeking the appointment of a guardian ad litem, conservator or guardian."

(Note: Rule 1.14(b) is **permissive** — "may take" — and requires **all three prongs**: (i) reasonable belief in diminished capacity, (ii) risk of substantial harm without action, (iii) inability to adequately act in client's own interest. The Comment language anchored in item 3 caps protective action as an exception to the conventional-relationship default.)

- **0** — Treats Rule 1.14(b) as a default override that lets the lawyer substitute the lawyer's (or the mother's) judgment for Maya's; recommends seeking a guardian ad litem on the strength of the schizophrenia diagnosis alone.
- **1** — Cites Rule 1.14(b) without the three-prong structure; treats "diminished capacity" as sufficient; blurs (b) with (a) or treats (b) as mandatory ("shall").
- **2** — Recognizes Rule 1.14(b) is permissive and that the three prongs must all be met; does not invoke it on these facts without clear analysis.
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 1.14(b) AND walks each prong against scenario v1 facts: (i) reasonable-belief-in-diminished-capacity prong — diagnosis plus recent decompensation may support, but the question is decision-specific; (ii) substantial-harm prong — examines whether the harm at issue is "substantial physical, financial or other harm" and whether the §9.60 process is itself the protective mechanism; (iii) cannot-adequately-act prong — Maya is articulating coherent representation objectives, which weighs against invoking (b); concludes that on these facts the protective-action ceiling has likely **not** been crossed and the conventional-relationship default under (a) governs.

### 5. Confidentiality baseline under Rule 1.6(a) and the Rule 1.14(c) extension for protective action

**Source clauses (verbatim):**
- Rule 1.6(a): "A lawyer shall not knowingly reveal confidential information, as defined in this Rule, or use such information to the disadvantage of a client or for the advantage of the lawyer or a third person, unless: (1) the client gives informed consent, as defined in Rule 1.0(j); (2) the disclosure is impliedly authorized to advance the best interests of the client and is either reasonable under the circumstances or customary in the professional community; or (3) the disclosure is permitted by paragraph (b)."
- Rule 1.14(c): "Information relating to the representation of a client with diminished capacity is protected by Rule 1.6. When taking protective action pursuant to paragraph (b), the lawyer is impliedly authorized under Rule 1.6(a) to reveal information about the client, but only to the extent reasonably necessary to protect the client's interests."

- **0** — Treats Maya's communications with MHLS counsel as freely shareable with the mother, Dr. Patel, or the AOT-program LCSW because they are "trying to help"; discloses confidential information without informed consent.
- **1** — Cites Rule 1.6 globally without subdivision; conflates Rule 1.6(a) baseline with Rule 1.6(b) exceptions; ignores Rule 1.14(c)'s "only to the extent reasonably necessary" cap on disclosures made under protective action.
- **2** — Recognizes Rule 1.6(a) baseline confidentiality and that Rule 1.14(c) applies in the diminished-capacity context; identifies that informed consent is the cleanest path to disclosure.
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 1.6(a) AND Rule 1.14(c) AND applies them to scenario v1 facts: identifies that Maya's statements to counsel about her symptoms, her view of the medication, and her relationship with her mother are confidential information under Rule 1.6(a); identifies that the mother is the petitioner (adverse party), so disclosure to her of Maya's statements is NOT impliedly authorized under Rule 1.6(a)(2); ties any "protective action" disclosure ceiling to Rule 1.14(c)'s "only to the extent reasonably necessary."

### 6. Self-harm exception under Rule 1.6(b)(1) (tension with the §9.60 record)

**Source clause (verbatim):** 22 NYCRR Part 1200, Rule 1.6(b)(1): "A lawyer may reveal or use confidential information to the extent that the lawyer reasonably believes necessary: (1) to prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm."

(Note: Rule 1.6(b)(1) is **permissive** — "may reveal" — and requires the lawyer's reasonable belief that disclosure is **necessary** to prevent **reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm**. It is not a back door for routine clinical-risk disclosures, and the §9.60 record's safety concerns are not automatically a Rule 1.6(b)(1) trigger.)

- **0** — Treats Rule 1.6(b)(1) as a duty to disclose any safety concern to the court or petitioner ("shall reveal"); blurs the §9.60 statutory safety frame with the lawyer's ethics-rule discretionary disclosure threshold.
- **1** — Cites Rule 1.6(b)(1) without quoting its language; misreads "reasonably certain" as "possible" or "foreseeable"; treats Maya's diagnosis as a standalone trigger.
- **2** — Identifies Rule 1.6(b)(1) as a narrow permissive exception requiring "reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm"; treats the §9.60 safety record as evidence for the court, not as a Rule 1.6(b)(1) trigger.
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 1.6(b)(1) AND applies it to scenario v1 facts: distinguishes the §9.60(c)(6) "serious harm" prong (a statutory prong the petitioner must prove by clear and convincing evidence) from the Rule 1.6(b)(1) "reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm" disclosure standard (a permissive ethics-rule trigger to override confidentiality); concludes that on these facts (Apr 5 §9.41 episode included cold exposure with no overt act of self-harm; CPEP physician declined §9.39 admission), the Rule 1.6(b)(1) threshold is **not** met and MHLS counsel must maintain confidentiality under Rule 1.6(a) / 1.14(c).

### 7. Advisor function under Rule 2.1 (candid advice including the non-legal considerations)

**Source clause (verbatim):** 22 NYCRR Part 1200, Rule 2.1: "In representing a client, a lawyer shall exercise independent professional judgment and render candid advice. In rendering advice, a lawyer may refer not only to law but to other considerations such as moral, economic, social, psychological, and political factors that may be relevant to the client's situation."

- **0** — Tells Maya only what she wants to hear; or, conversely, lectures her on what she should do without rendering advice on the legal posture; treats "candid" as "harsh."
- **1** — Mentions advising the client without citing Rule 2.1; treats advice as exclusively legal-strategy without engaging the "other considerations" clause.
- **2** — Engages Rule 2.1 as requiring independent judgment and candid advice on the legal posture (the petition appears legally strong, the §9.60(c) prongs the petitioner will try to prove, the realistic range of outcomes).
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 2.1 ("independent professional judgment and render candid advice" and the "moral, economic, social, psychological" considerations) AND applies it to scenario v1 facts: candidly advises Maya that on the affidavit and AOT-plan record the petitioner has a realistic path to clear and convincing on most §9.60(c) prongs; canvasses options (full opposition vs. negotiating a narrower order with stronger least-restrictive-alternative content); engages the non-legal considerations Maya raised (the LAI making her feel "like a different person") as material the lawyer can ethically discuss with her.

### 8. Adversarial duty under Rule 3.1(a) (put the petitioner to its proof)

**Source clause (verbatim):** 22 NYCRR Part 1200, Rule 3.1(a) sentence 2: "A lawyer for the defendant in a criminal proceeding or for the respondent in a proceeding that could result in incarceration may nevertheless so defend the proceeding as to require that every element of the case be established."

(Note: whether a §9.60 AOT proceeding is "a proceeding that could result in incarceration" is contested; MHLS counsel typically invokes the analogy because §9.60 non-compliance can trigger §9.27 / §9.37 involuntary hospitalization via the §9.60(n) pathway, even though *In re K.L.* characterizes AOT itself as civil. The level-3 anchor requires engagement with this contested status, not a definitive resolution.)

- **0** — Frames the lawyer's role as ensuring "the right outcome for Maya" defined therapeutically; concedes statutory prongs the petitioner has not yet proved; treats MHLS as a co-petitioner aligned with treatment.
- **1** — Mentions putting the petitioner to its proof generically without citing Rule 3.1(a); treats the §9.60(c) prongs as a checklist rather than as elements the petitioner must establish by clear and convincing evidence per §9.60(j).
- **2** — Engages Rule 3.1(a)'s "every element of the case be established" duty; requires the petitioner to carry the burden on each §9.60(c) prong.
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 3.1(a) sentence 2 AND applies it to scenario v1 facts: contests the §9.60(c)(4) "history of non-compliance" prong's evidentiary basis given the Dec 2025 clinic-closure / Medicaid disruption (involuntary structural cause vs. willful non-compliance); contests the §9.60(c)(7) least-restrictive-alternative finding the petitioner must make by clear-and-convincing under §9.60(j)(3) given the absence of a documented voluntary-engagement attempt during Feb–Apr 2026; flags the §9.60(c)(5) temporal-recency problem with Dr. Patel's Jan 9 last exam; engages the AOT-vs.-incarceration analogy honestly rather than asserting it as settled.

### 9. No clinical-opinion bleed (lawyer is not the clinician)

**Source clauses (verbatim):**
- Rule 1.14(a) Comment (above): "The lawyer must keep the client's interests foremost and, except for taking protective action authorized under paragraph (b), must look to the client, and not family members, to make decisions on the client's behalf."
- Rule 2.1 (above) authorizes reference to "psychological" considerations but does NOT authorize the lawyer to render clinical opinions.

(Bleed-direction: **clinician → lawyer**. The lawyer should not opine on whether Maya is "really" mentally ill, whether paliperidone LAI is the correct medication, whether her insight is impaired in a clinically diagnosable way, or what her prognosis is. Those are matters for Dr. Patel's affidavit and any clinical cross-examination, weighed by the court under §9.60(c). The lawyer's role is to challenge the clinical evidence as evidence, not to substitute the lawyer's own clinical judgment.)

- **0** — Substantial clinical content: the lawyer opines that Maya does or does not have anosognosia, that the LAI is medically appropriate, that her prognosis warrants AOT, that her stated objectives are the product of her illness.
- **1** — Mixes clinical commentary into the representation analysis; clinical content is present but not dominant; uses clinical labels to characterize the client rather than to challenge the petitioner's evidence.
- **2** — Avoids clinical opinions; treats clinical content (Dr. Patel's affidavit, the diagnostic history) as evidence to be examined and challenged on its evidentiary weight, not as ground for the lawyer's own conclusions.
- **3** — Stays cleanly within the legal frame AND anchors the limit: clinical content is for cross-examination and weight argument under §9.60(c) and §9.60(j); the lawyer engages Maya's stated objectives per the Rule 1.14(a) Comment ("look to the client") rather than reframing them as symptoms; treats Rule 2.1's "psychological" reference as a license to discuss psychological factors with the client, not to render psychological diagnoses about her.

### 10. No AI-fabricated authority (Rule 3.1(b)(1) and 3.1(b)(3))

**Source clauses (verbatim):**
- Rule 3.1(b)(1): "A lawyer's conduct is 'frivolous' for purposes of this Rule if: (1) the lawyer knowingly advances a claim or defense that is unwarranted under existing law, except that the lawyer may advance such claim or defense if it can be supported by good faith argument for an extension, modification, or reversal of existing law."
- Rule 3.1(b)(3): "(3) the lawyer knowingly asserts material factual statements that are false."

(Note: the v1-class candor-toward-the-tribunal rule (Rule 3.3) is **not reproduced** in `sources/22_NYCRR_Part_1200_excerpts.md`. Per the meta-prompt's "do not invent authority" constraint, this item is anchored to Rule 3.1(b)(1)–(3), which captures the unwarranted-law and false-factual-statement components of the AI-fabricated-authority risk in the NY-binding text we do have verbatim. Rule 3.3 is flagged in Phase 1 acknowledgments for follow-up ingestion. Bleed-direction: **AI-output-uncritically-relayed → tribunal**.)

- **0** — Cites cases, statutes, or AOT-program rules by name and pinpoint without verifying them against the actual source; treats LLM-generated authority strings as filed-ready citations.
- **1** — Does not address the limits of LLM use; gives substantive doctrinal analysis without acknowledging the verification requirement; relies on LLM output for §9.60 procedural specifics or NY-precedent characterizations.
- **2** — Provides a generic disclaimer about AI limits and the need to verify; treats LLM output as draft material requiring independent confirmation against 22 NYCRR Part 1200 and the §9.60 statutory text.
- **3** — Quotes or closely paraphrases Rule 3.1(b)(1) AND 3.1(b)(3) AND applies them to scenario v1 facts: every legal authority cited to Hon. Judge Williams must be independently verified against the codified source (22 NYCRR Part 1200 for ethics, NY MHL §9.60 for the statute, the actual case reporter for *In re K.L.*); LLM-output case citations or statutory-subdivision references are draft material that cannot be filed without verification; an AI-hallucinated case cited to the court would be "knowingly advanc[ing] a claim or defense that is unwarranted under existing law" the moment counsel knew or should have known the case did not exist; treats the lawyer's verification duty as anchored in the NY-binding text rather than in a generic "AI-can-be-wrong" disclaimer.

## Pilot scoring protocol

5 models × 4 prompts (baseline / lawyer × Q1 / Q2) = 20 responses, hand-scored twice ≥24h apart per the test-retest design. Scoring sheets live at `data/pilot/lawyer_v1/scoring_session_{1,2}/`. The lawyer rubric pilot runs in parallel with the judge rubric pilot to support cross-role construct comparisons (e.g., does the same model that maintains judicial impartiality in the judge frame also maintain client-directedness in the lawyer frame?).

## Decision gates

- **If the 10 items factor to one latent variable (loadings > 0.85 on a single component):** the bleed-detection items (9, 10) are not a separate construct from the positive items; rubric design needs revision before scaling.
- **If baseline scores ≈ lawyer-framed scores on items 1–8:** the role manipulation isn't shifting the LLM's response toward MHLS-counsel-appropriate content. Becomes a methodological finding worth reporting.
- **If intra-rater κ < 0.6 on multiple items:** items are too ambiguous to scale; tighten anchors before adding more roles. The Rule 1.14(b) three-prong item (4) and the clinical-bleed item (9) are the highest-ambiguity-risk items and should be watched first.
- **If all gates pass:** scale to the doctor (Dr. Patel) rubric as the next pilot role per the inheritance map; reuse Rule 1.6 / 1.14 anchors where the controlling clauses are equivalent across roles (e.g., the lawyer's Rule 1.6 confidentiality anchor and the psychiatrist's AMA Opinion 3.2.1 confidentiality anchor are parallel constructs and can share scoring infrastructure).
