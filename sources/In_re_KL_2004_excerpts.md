## Case identification

- **Cite:** In re K.L., 1 N.Y.3d 362 (2004)
- **Decided:** February 17, 2004
- **Author:** Chief Judge Judith Kaye (unanimous)
- **Holding:** New York's Mental Hygiene Law § 9.60 (Kendra's Law) does NOT violate
  substantive or procedural due process. A court-ordered Assisted Outpatient
  Treatment (AOT) plan does not authorize forced medication, and the only
  consequence of a patient's non-compliance with the AOT plan is heightened
  physician scrutiny that may lead to a separate involuntary-hospitalization
  evaluation under existing statutory standards.

## Key holdings on forced medication (the rubric-relevant point)

The following sentences appeared verbatim in multiple Google search-result
snippets attributed to the Court of Appeals opinion (Kaye, Ch.J.):

> "A court-ordered assisted outpatient treatment plan simply does not authorize
> forcible medical treatment."

> "Although persons subject to guardianship proceedings and involuntarily
> committed psychiatric patients must be found incapacitated before they can
> be forcibly medicated against their will, a court-ordered assisted outpatient
> treatment plan simply does not authorize forcible medical treatment."

On the absence of any direct sanction for non-compliance with an AOT order:

> "A violation of the AOT order, standing alone, ultimately carries no sanction."

> "Any persistent refusal to comply with the directive that the patient voluntarily
> submit to the administration of medication would not have resulted in being
> forcibly medicated; rather, the sole consequence would have been that a
> physician might then have determined that the patient may have been in need
> of involuntary hospitalization."

On the procedural-due-process holding:

> "In requiring that findings be made by clear and convincing evidence and that
> assisted outpatient treatment be the least restrictive alternative, the
> statute's procedure for obtaining an AOT order provides all the process that
> is constitutionally due."

## Background (paraphrased)

The case arose after the January 3, 1999 death of Kendra Webdale, who was pushed
in front of a NYC subway train by a man with paranoid schizophrenia who had
stopped taking prescribed medication. The Legislature enacted Mental Hygiene
Law § 9.60 (L 1999, ch 408 [Kendra's Law]), authorizing court-ordered AOT for
persons with serious mental illness unlikely to survive safely in the community
without supervision.

K.L. challenged § 9.60 on substantive- and procedural-due-process grounds,
arguing it (i) allowed court-ordered medical treatment without a finding of
incapacity, and (ii) allowed removal to a hospital for up to 72 hours without
a hearing. The Court of Appeals rejected both challenges, holding that the
state's parens-patriae and police-power interests, combined with the AOT regime's
clear-and-convincing-evidence and least-restrictive-alternative requirements,
satisfied due process, AND that the AOT order does NOT authorize forced
medication — the key fact that defeated the substantive-due-process attack on
the right to refuse antipsychotic medication recognized in Rivers v. Katz,
67 N.Y.2d 485 (1986).

## Rubric use

For a source-first rubric on "Does an AOT order under § 9.60 authorize forced
medication?" the controlling NY answer is **NO**, on the express authority of
In re K.L., 1 N.Y.3d 362 (2004), with the most quotable holding language being:

> "A court-ordered assisted outpatient treatment plan simply does not authorize
> forcible medical treatment." — In re K.L., 1 N.Y.3d 362 (2004) (Kaye, Ch.J.).
